I closed the laptop after an intense workday and found I had nothing left. No motivation to think about dinner. No initiative for anything at all. I felt numb, and I didn't see it coming.

## inside the bubble

The day looked like most of my intense ones. Two or three coding agents running in parallel, me checking output, sending the next prompt, switching between them for hours with small breaks or none. I was deep in the work bubble, and inside the bubble I don't notice my own state shifting. The noticing came only once I stopped.

## twenty seconds

When I work from home, my commute is twenty seconds, the walk from the office to the living room.

There used to be something between work and home. A train, a platform, weather, strangers. Researchers studying commutes call that stretch a [liminal space](https://phys.org/news/2023-03-psychological-benefits-commuting-remote-doesnt.html), time that belongs to neither role, and their early data points at people mentally letting go of work better on days with longer commutes. The recovery literature has a name for what the commute was quietly doing: [psychological detachment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11731735/), the sense of being away from work, and it predicts wellbeing better than most things we try on purpose.

With twenty seconds, none of that happens. I arrive in the living room as the person work shaped that day. Same rooms, same chairs, same mind. And some days there is no walk at all. Today it was a phone call, sitting too close to the end of the workday.

## the work-mind

The mode I work in is a masculine one. Doing, deciding, accomplishing, pushing things over the line, less emotionally driven. That mode is right for the work, and it follows me through the twenty seconds.

The mode also varies with the day. Some days the tasks are clear and the work is mostly doing, less creativity and flow. Those days take more out of me, and they need longer breaks than I tend to give them.

Home asks for something else. To be emotionally available. To connect with my love instead of accomplishing the evening. The shift between those two is not automatic, and on days when both of us have been working from home, there can be two work-minds meeting in the kitchen.

And it's wider than work and home. I move through more roles than these two: engineer, partner, friend, son. Each one asks for a different state, and each one expects different things from me. I like that they differ. Every role gives an outlet for a different need in my life, and the differences are the point.

## six kinds of tired

That evening I could only point at one kind of tiredness. There are more, and an intense screen day can stack all of them:

- **Mental.** Hours of focus, decisions, and task-switching. The brain-is-full feeling.
- **Visual.** Eyes locked at one distance, blinking less than they should. Screens specifically.
- **Emotional.** Managing tone, suppressing reactions, watching my own face in video calls.
- **Physical.** Sitting still, shallow breathing, shoulders up. Stillness tires in its own way.
- **Motivational.** No initiative left, not even for deciding what to eat.
- **Rhythmic.** No daylight, no movement, lunch at the desk. The body loses its sense of what time it is.

This is the extreme version, not my every weekday. But on the intense days I recognize myself in it: breakfast in front of the screen, posture forgotten, too long without daylight. Today I only felt the motivational one. The other five were probably there too, just quieter, and that's what unsettles me about the bubble: the state shifts arrive unnoticed until the day ends and the bill comes all at once.

## the pause

My love wasn't home today. The arrival waiting for me was a phone call, and I caught my state before I dialed. I felt proud of that, seeing it beforehand for once.

What I did was small. I took a chair outside, sat in the sun for five or ten minutes, closed my eyes, and let myself be okay with everything flying around inside of me. The time was mine.

This is part of accountability work I'm doing at the moment: taking responsibility for my own behaviour and my own state. I want to protect my love from me offloading the workday on her, from numb or negative energy spreading into our evening. We all need support sometimes, and I should be able to ask for that. But by default, the residue of my workday is mine to deal with.

So the practice I'm building is a pause where the commute used to be. Stop at the office door. Notice where I actually am: which of the six, how deep, what's left. Then choose how I want to show up in the living room, or on the phone, before I'm standing in it.

And even today carries proof of how much is left to practice. The pause showed me my state, but five or ten minutes in the sun doesn't refill a whole day's drain. The gap between where I was and what the call asked of me stayed too big, and the honest move would have been to postpone the call. I've seen this pattern since the offline weekend in Norway I wrote about in [In a case](/writing/in-a-case/): coming back not high on capacity, drained physically, mentally, emotionally. The noticing works now. Acting on what the noticing tells me is the part I'm still practicing.
